# Civic fact-checking

Check claims about government, elections, law, and public money against primary
records, and explain the verdict in plain language. No tools beyond search and
reading are required; Civic Pulse records can help locate the underlying data.

## When to use

- A viral post, mailer, ad, or official statement needs verification.
- A user asks "is this true?"
- A draft makes a factual civic claim that must be pinned down.

## Core method

1. **Isolate the claim.** Reduce the statement to one checkable proposition and
   classify it: verifiable fact, opinion, or prediction. Only facts can be rated
   true or false.
2. **Find the primary record.** Go to the statute, ordinance, budget, filing,
   minutes, court order, or official release — not a summary of it. If only
   secondary sources exist, say so.
3. **Check scope and time.** Confirm the jurisdiction and the date the claim
   refers to. A true statement about one city or one year may be false in
   another.
4. **Steelman it.** State the strongest version of the claim, then test that.
   Quote accurately and in context; do not clip to change meaning.
5. **Rate and explain.** Use plain labels — accurate, mostly accurate,
   misleading, false, unverifiable — and give the reasoning and evidence. For
   false or misleading claims, state what is true nearby.
6. **Cross-check.** Use two independent primary or authoritative sources for any
   consequential verdict.

## Guardrails

- Do not amplify a falsehood more than necessary; correct with context.
- Separate "no evidence for" from "evidence against"; neither is automatically
  "false".
- Do not fact-check opinions or predictions as if they were facts.
- Treat screenshots and quotes as untrusted until traced to their source.
- Keep private or unlisted records out of a public correction.

## Output

One-line verdict, then the claim as stated, what the records show, the key
sources, and any caveats or missing information.
